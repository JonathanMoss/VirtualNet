CHAPTER 1
SECTION 1 – SECURITY
THE RADIO WAR
1.
The range, efficiency and traffic capacity of modern radios provide an excellent
method of communication. Unfortunately, radio is also the least secure means of
communications. The unauthorised interception and recording of radio transmissions is
impossible to detect or prevent.
2.
Our radio communications are a constant source of valuable intelligence to our
enemies, both in peace and war. Information collected in peacetime is available for long
term analysis by a potential enemy and is extremely useful for the planning of operations
against us in time of war. This task is simplified by our often-unwitting relaxation of security
precautions during training exercises, actions that would be considered unthinkable in war.
Inferior security and operating procedures practised in peacetime are unlikely to be
discarded or rectified immediately prior to operations in time of crisis.
3.
Enemy response to security breaches made in wartime can be expected to be
immediate. Even secure radio communications can provide considerable intelligence from
characteristic patterns, traffic flow and the location of transmitters obtainable by direction
finding (DF).
4.
It must be assumed that every radio transmission made during training exercises of
on operations will be intercepted and evaluated by hostile signal intelligence (SIGINT)
agencies, and the resulting intelligence ultimately used against us.
INTERCEPTED INFORMATION
5.
This constant monitoring of our radio communications and the study and
interpretation of the various traffic characteristics provide the enemy with vital current
information concerning our fighting capabilities.
6.
Intercepted information, when analysed and correlated with existing data from other
sources, can provide intelligence of sufficient value to influence significantly enemy
command decisions.

SECURITY OF INFORMATION
8.
Whether transmitted during training or on operations, all information of a long- or
short-term nature likely to assist an enemy in wartime should be encoded, whenever
possible, prior to transmission on insecure radio. It is mandatory to encode:
a.

Grid references or place names identifying locations of friendly forces.

b.
Grid references or place names identifying locations of enemy forces if the
information is gained by stealth. When both friendly and enemy forces are aware
that contact has been made, in other words after an exchange of fire has taken
place, contact reports may be sent in clear.
c.

Lines of Departure.

d.

FUPs and routes.

e.

Harbours and hides.

f.

Timings.

g.
etc).

Future intentions (including ‘Warning Orders’, ‘Orders’ and ‘No Move Before’

9.
Once sensitive information has been transmitted in clear, there is no guaranteed
period of safety and it must be assumed that the enemy reaction will be immediate.
10.
Each transmission must be carefully considered and the risk calculated. The rival
priorities of security and speed are to be carefully weighed and balanced against the
enemy’s ability to intercept and exploit the transmitted information.

COMMUNICATIONS SECURITY
11.
Communications Security (COMSEC) is the collective title for the measures taken
to protect all aspects of communications in order to deny information and intelligence to an
enemy.
TRANSMISSION SECURITY
12.
Transmission Security is that component of COMSEC which includes all measures
designed to protect our radio transmissions from enemy interception, analysis and
deception.
a.
Interception. Interception is the act of searching for, listening to and
recording radio communications and electronic transmissions for the purpose of
obtaining information.
b.
Analysis. Analysis is the examination and interpretation of intercepted radio
communications traffic and electronic transmissions for the purpose of obtaining
intelligence.
c.
Deception. Deception is the introduction of a false transmission into a
communications system, by imitating an authentic transmission, with the intention to
deceive or create confusion.
13.
Other components of COMSEC and Transmission Security not directly related to
voice procedure are not considered in this pamphlet.
DEFENCE AGAINST INTERCEPTION
14.
The use of radio is part of an overall Emission Control (EMCON) policy and is a
command function decided at the highest level.
15.
No radio transmission, wherever it is made, can be regarded as safe from
interception. It is important to remember that intercept receivers are designed to receive
weaker signals at greater distances than standard receivers.
16.
Maximum defence against interception and DF can be achieved if the following
protective measures are taken:
a.
Radio Silence. Radio silence is the only conclusive defence against
interception. Instructions and procedures regarding the imposition, breaking and
lifting of radio silence must be rigidly adhered to if the expected protection they can
provide against enemy interception is to be attained. The lifting of radio silence does
not allow the radio to be used indiscriminately.
b.
Electronic Silence. Orders for the imposition of electronic silence may be
passed over the radio by the use of a codeword issued by the general staff.
c.
Avoiding Unnecessary Transmissions. The necessity for each radio
transmission should be carefully considered; radio is often used habitually when
adequate alternative methods of communication are available. Having decided to
make a transmission, called stations should be limited to essential addressees only

to avoid unnecessary replies and acknowledgements. The checking of
communications and associated transmitter tuning and testing should be reduced to
an absolute minimum.
d.
Transmission Length. The longer a transmission the more vulnerable it is
to intercept and DF. Individual transmissions should not exceed 20 seconds and,
whenever possible, be terminated with the proword OUT.
e.
Procedure. Poor procedure can increase transmission time and cause a
series of unnecessary transmissions requesting clarification. Strict adherence to
correct message procedure and communication drills, combined with good net
discipline, will minimize transmission time and reduce vulnerability to interception
and DF.
f.
Changing Frequency. Continuity of interception can be broken by changing
frequency. Whenever possible, the instruction to change frequency should be
encoded or passed by secure means – this is important when the change occurs at
an unpredictable time. Frequencies are never to be passed in clear over insecure
radio.
g.
Use of Alternative Means of Communications. The maximum use should
be made of communications other than radio, particularly for mundane
administrative traffic.
DEFENCE AGAINST ANALYSIS
17.
The following measures should be taken to render enemy traffic analysis more
difficult and any resulting intelligence less reliable:
a.
Calling and Answering. The greater the number of stations included in a
call, the more potential intelligence there is available to an analyst to decide the
composition, size and level of a net. For this reason, all station calls and large,
multi-station calls are unduly detrimental to security; their use should be confined to
exceptional, unavoidable circumstances. Collective calls have the advantage of
brevity without the need to divulge each callsign in the initial call but, used
excessively, subsequent answering calls will soon provide the analyst with their
grouping and identity.
b.
Callsign Omission. After the initial calling and answering calls have been
made and the identities of the calling and called stations have been satisfactorily
established, callsigns should be omitted entirely unless circumstances demand
otherwise. In addition to denying the analyst useful repetition and confirmation of
callsigns, overall transmission time is reduced.
c.
Locations. Locations, whether our own or those of an enemy, are the most
sensitive information we send over radio and are an unrivalled source of intelligence
to an enemy; an analyst can invariably relate un-encoded enemy locations to our
own positions. This is often due to our inadvertent disclosure of associated plain
language, in either the same or other messages, combined with his knowledge or
our tactical doctrine. Furthermore, an enemy commander, given our knowledge of
his positions, can take appropriate counter action. As a general rule, all locations
should be encoded.

d.
Plain Language. Plain language information of an obviously sensitive nature
must be encoded prior to transmission over insecure radio. Every effort should also
be made to identify and encode plain language information which may, in isolation,
appear innocuous but when assembled with other similar information by an analyst
becomes classified. Ideally, with the sensible use of encoded vocabulary codes,
transmissions should contain the minimum of exploitable plain language text.
e.
Avoiding Compromise. Plain language reference to callsigns, address
groups, low level codes or other security aids is forbidden and must be constantly
guarded against if the security value and protection they offer is not to be
compromised. Linkage or compromise enabling continuity between old and new
operating frequencies must be avoided.
f.
Standardization. Idiosyncrasies of speech and operating technique enable
the analyst, aided by a comprehensive filing system, to recognise an individual, his
unit and its expected role. The aim of every radio user should be to remain
anonymous. The names of personnel or units must never be transmitted in clear.
Unique personal expressions and mannerisms, unit jargon, mention of specialist
vehicles or equipment must be avoided.
DEFENCE AGAINST DECEPTION
18.
The enemy will rarely attempt deception during peacetime, but almost exclusively
reserve its use for time of war to ensure maximum impact. Unsuspecting stations
preoccupied with urgent tactical matters are particularly prone to this method of attack.
The best defences against enemy deception are:
a.
Good Procedures. The success of enemy deception relies on his ability to
appear inconspicuous, which is mainly dependent on his knowledge of our
operational procedures.
b.
Constant Vigilance. Radio users and operators must remain alert to
irregularities in procedure, unfamiliar speech and unexpected transmissions of
dubious origin. Subtle use of previously recorded transmission of our own nets,
played back by an enemy, can cause confusion and endanger our own troops;
these may not necessarily be on the same net or within a similar timeframe. Special
care must also be taken to identify and ignore counterfeit transmissions made solely
to solicit answering calls, normally for DF purposes, when radio silence is in force.
Whenever deception is recognized, or suspected, it should be reported immediately
by secure means as a EW/JAMREP.
c.
Authentication. The correct and timely use of authorized authentication
systems helps protect our nets against enemy communications deception. The
requirement for good authentication practices is less apparent in training but will
contribute significantly to combat survival and effectiveness in time of war.

AIDS TO SECURITY
19.
There are procedural aids which can considerably enhance transmission security.
The degree and period of security protection afforded by these aids is greatly dependent
on their correct use; this requires a proper understanding of their potential and also their
limitations. Abuse or misuse of security aids will quickly counter their security value and,
equally dangerously, instil a false sense of security.
20.
Unofficial, locally designed codes or adaptation of official codes, however wellintentioned, will not deceive a cryptanalyst; only officially authorized codes are to be used.
Aids to security are:
a.

Authorized low level codes.

b.

Authentication.

c.

Codewords.

d.

Address groups.

e.

Callsigns.

f.

Net identification signs.

LOW LEVEL CODES
21.
Low level codes are designed to provide security protection for sensitive, short-term
tactical information where speed and simplicity of operation are the overriding
considerations. When time and circumstances permit, secure communications systems or
alternative physical methods should be used.
22.
There are various types of code available, some of which are for theatre use or for
specific tasks; these, when required, will be issued with operating instructions for their use.
The only current authorized low-level code in general use is BATCO (Battle Code).
23.
BATCO is designed to encode numerals only, but it may also be used, in
conjunction with a numbered vocabulary code, to send plain text. Spelling of words is
permitted with BATCO but only under the rules laid down. The inclusion of letters in the
vocabulary cards for the subsequent encoding of words will destroy the security of the
code and is forbidden. Operating instructions for BATCO are given at Annex A.
24.
What to Encode. Apart from the rules laid down in above, there can be no hard
and fast guidance on what else in a message exploitable text in any particular
circumstance may be. Military judgement must be exercised in every case to answer the
following questions:
a.
How much information am I prepared to let the enemy obtain from my radio
traffic?
b.

How might the enemy exploit the information I am about to transmit?

EXPLOITABLE TEXT
25.
Exploitable text is that which provides useful information for an enemy to use in a
timely way (his response time can be expected to be as little as a few minutes) to our
disadvantage; it includes locations (both place names and map references) and timings,
assembly areas, start lines, unit states and indications of intentions. Such indications
include ‘warning orders’, ‘orders’, ‘no move before’, etc and their exploitability must be
judged according to the tactical situation at the time. For example, in the heat of battle,
‘orders’ may be commonplace and of no significance to an enemy; on the other hand,
during a lull, a ‘warning order’ may be the key to his discovering the time and place of a
counter-attack. For added guidance the policy about what may or may not normally be
sent by unprotected means will be laid down by the theatre/command HQ concerned.
26.
All exploitable text must be encoded whenever the situation permits. If the delay
imposed by encoding a message, or parts of a message, will prejudice the operation more
than the plain text transmission will prejudice its security, then the message may be sent in
plain text at the discretion of the originator. However, he must understand that he may be
called upon to justify his decision.
REAL OR NON-EXERCISE TRAFFIC
27.
In training there is a need to differentiate between exercise play and events which
require action outside the context of the exercise; conventionally this is known as “real”
activity. Messages associated with real activity are just as exploitable as any other and
care must be taken to ensure that security is not unduly prejudiced by the urgency of such
situations. In order to highlight the differences between real and other messages, the
proword NO PLAY is used. Its meaning is defined in Annex D.
AUTHENTICATION
28.
Authentication is a security aid designed to protect our communications against
deception from fraudulent transmissions by unauthorized persons.
29.
Authentication Systems. The type of authentication system in use will usually
depend upon the type of net, i.e. unit or formation level. Challenge and reply authentication
is common to both unit and formation nets, transmission or self-authentication is normally
confined to formation or specialized nets.
a.
Challenge and Reply. This form of authentication works on a simple grid
system and is available to both unit and formation nets. The authentication grids are
printed on the current code tables and are valid for the duration of those tables.
Challenge and reply systems of authentication (such as BATCO) may not be used
for self-authentication, which is a method of establishing authenticity by sending
both the challenge and reply.
b.
Transmission Authentication. Transmission authentication is used for the
authentication of transmissions when it is impossible, or impracticable, to use
challenge and reply. This authentication system is most commonly used on secure
nets at brigade level and above. It is always produced centrally and will be issued
as required, together with instructions for its use. Transmission authentication must
never be used in preference to challenge and reply. BATCO authentication tables
should never be used for transmission authentication.

30.
The rules concerning the occasions when authentication should be used, with

CODEWORDS
31.
A codeword is a single word with a pre-arranged meaning, used to establish a
condition, an alert, or to initiate the implementation of a plan or operation. Codewords are
prepared and issued by the Operations Staff. Their meanings are always classified and
are intended to be used once only as an executive order. They may therefore be sent in
clear. Codewords are rarely used in connection with communications, other than for
conditions of electronic silence. See also Chapter 4 Section 3.
32.
Radio Appointment Titles. Radio appointment titles are not codewords and are
authorized for combined and joint working as described in ACP 125. With one exception,
appointment titles are not authorized for use on land force single service radio nets; the
exception being SUNRAY, which is to be used for the Commander at any level, qualified
by ‘MY’, ‘YOUR’, etc callsign and address group. SUNRAY MINOR may also be used
when referring to the second-in-command.
ADDRESS GROUPS
33.
An address group is a unique, daily-changing group of four random letters assigned
to disguise a command, formation, unit or sub-unit. Address groups are classified when
connected with their meaning.
34.
Use of Address Groups. The use of address groups should be kept to a minimum
and never qualified. Address groups are used:
a.
In place of clear names and addresses in voice conversations and
messages, except when referring to a station in the same unit or on the same net.
b.
As a temporary callsign, when a station joins a net for which he has no
allocated callsign.
c.
To refer, as required, by implication and context, either to the headquarters
to which the address group is allotted, or to the whole formation, unit or sub-unit
served by that headquarters.
35.
When used, address groups are pronounced phonetically and, except in the
address component of a formal message, are preceded by the proword ADDRESS
GROUP.
CALLSIGNS
36.
A callsign is a combination of letters and figures which identifies a communications
facility, an organization, or an individual on a radio net. Its primary use is to establish and
maintain communications. It is a 2-element, part-changing and part-fixed system. It is
described in detail in Chapter 2 Section 2.

NET IDENTIFICATION SIGN
37.
A net identification sign (NIS) is a random, twice daily-changing, two-letter group
(with the exception of CC) allotted to a radio net to provide a concealed reference to the
identity of that net. The NIS should be used whenever it is necessary to distinguish
between radio nets or, in conjunction with a callsign, to identify a station on a specific net.
The NIS may be used to avoid confusion when:
a.
Referring, either by implication or context, to a station from another net by
using that net's NIS in conjunction with the station’s callsign.
b.
A station requires to join or operate briefly on a net for which it has no
allotted callsign or callsign indicator. The joining station should use the NIS,
together with the callsign from the net on which he normally operates, as a
temporary callsign.
c.
Establishing or re-establishing communications, as a tuning call or as part of
the initial transmission.
d.
Identifying stations from another net which are causing interference on the
operating frequency.
38.
Use of the NIS degrades the security value gained by the allocation of similar
patterns of callsigns to each net, and therefore should not be used indiscriminately. This is
especially relevant after a frequency change within the period for which the same NIS
remains valid if continuity of analysis is to be avoided.
39.
Extra care should be taken never to accept the NIS as proof of a station’s identity if
it is possible to use authentication procedure.
SECURITY ON LAND-LINE COMMUNICATIONS
40.
So far this Section has described the measures that are assessed as necessary to
ensure a satisfactory level of security on radio communications. It should be recognized,
however, that there is also a threat to the security of messages that pass-through land-line
communications.
41.

The term land-line can be defined as follows:
a.
Local Land-Line. Local land-line is that which is laid by the host resident
unit occupying a defended position. Local land-line should be physically protected at
all times and communication confined to the local unit area.
b.
Area Land-Line. Area land-line is part of an area system which may either
extend over radio relay circuits such as BT telephone systems or where land-line is
laid over an area which is not adequately protected such as between
Squadron/Company Headquarters and Battlegroup Headquarters.

SECTION 2 – ACCURACY
GENERAL
44.
Voice procedure is designed to provide the fastest and most accurate method of
speech transmission. All messages should be pre-planned, brief and straightforward.
Ideally, messages should be written down: even brief notes reduce the risk of error.
Messages should be constructed clearly and logically in order not to confuse the recipient.
Where it is impracticable to write brief notes the entire transmission should be thought
through before operating the Pressel.
SPEECH TECHNIQUE
45.
Proper use of audio equipment and clear, concise speech over the radio can
ultimately decide whether transmissions are successfully received and understood at the
first attempt.
a.
Use of Audio Equipment. In many situations, particularly in noisy or difficult
conditions, the use of headsets fitted with a noise cancelling microphone is
preferable to loudspeakers and handsets: a headset will aid concentration and the
audibility of the incoming signal. The double-sided, noise cancelling microphone is
designed to cancel out surrounding noise, for example engine noise or gunfire,
allowing speech entering on one side to pass freely. The microphone should be as
close to the mouth as possible.
b.
Method of Speech. The key words to remember are rhythm, speed, volume
and pitch (RSVP):
(1)
Rhythm. Use short sentences divided into sensible phrases which
maintain a natural rhythm; they should not be spoken word by word.
(2)
Speed. Speak slightly slower than for normal conversation. Where a
message is to be written down by the recipients, or in difficult conditions,
extra time should be allowed to compensate for the receiving station
experiencing the worst conditions. Speed of transmission is easily adjusted
by increasing or decreasing the length of pauses between phrases, as
opposed to altering the gaps between words; the latter will create an
unnatural, halted style of speech which is difficult to understand.
(3)
Volume. Speak quietly when using whisper facilities, otherwise the
volume should be as for normal conversation. Shouting causes distortion.
(4)
Pitch. The voice should be pitched slightly higher than for normal
conversation to improve clarity.

SECTION 3 – DISCIPLINE
GENERAL
64.
Radio discipline is a fundamental ingredient of voice procedure without which a
radio net cannot function efficiently. In addition to lowering communications efficiency and
accuracy, inadequate radio discipline can result in a serious degradation of security
standards.
65.
It is the commander’s responsibility to impose and maintain discipline on a
radio net. They may choose to exercise that responsibility through the control station. All
radio nets or links must have a designated control station. In the absence of instructions to
the contrary, the control station is that station serving the senior headquarters or location.
66.
During difficult conditions, net efficiency can deteriorate even more rapidly if poor
operating standards are permitted by the control station.
RULES FOR RADIO DISCIPLINE
67.
The following rules for radio discipline are mandatory on all radio nets, where every
station must:
a.

Always:
(1)

Use correct voice procedure.

(2)
Maintain a constant listening radio watch, unless specific instructions
or permission has been received to the contrary. This requires that at least
one person be nominated to monitor the radio regardless of the
circumstances. All aspects of voice procedure are based on the assumption
that stations will respond to calls immediately.
(3)

Ensure that the correct frequency is in use.

(4)

Answer calls in the correct order and without delay.

(5)
Listen carefully before transmitting to ensure that the frequency is
clear and, where possible, allow for stations which cannot be heard.
(6)

Release the Pressel switch promptly.

(7)
On releasing the Pressel switch, ensure that the radio returns to the
receive condition.

SECTION 2 - CALLSIGNS
GENERAL
4.
A callsign is a combination of letters and figures used to identify a communications
station, an organization, or an individual on a radio net. A station which requires to contact
one or more other stations on a net does so using the allotted callsigns.
5.
Letters used in callsigns are always pronounced phonetically and figures are always
pronounced digit by digit, without the prowords I SPELL and FIGURES: for example A12B
is spoken as ‘ALPHA one two BRAVO’. Unless there is a need to avoid confusion,
callsigns in the texts of messages should be transmitted without a proword. Where
confusion is likely the proword CALLSIGN is to be used.
6.
On land force nets, callsigns are to be used in full and include the callsign indicator
(CI) and any suffix. They are never to be abbreviated.
CALLSIGN SYSTEM
7.
The callsign system for general use on land force nets comprises a permanently
allocated or fixed callsign prefixed by a daily changing callsign indicator. The fixed callsign
element comprises two digits in the range 1Ø - 14, 2Ø - 24, 3Ø - 34 and 4Ø – 44
respectively and may include a suffix A-F. This system is to be used on single service nets
and all units’ nets.
8.
Each station and certain appointments on a net are allocated a fixed callsign for
permanent use. All callsigns fall within a fixed numerical allocation from the range:
Ø: 1Ø - 14: 2Ø - 24: 3Ø - 34: and 4Ø - 44: with suffixes A-F as required.
In general:
a.

The callsign Ø is allocated to the control station on every net.

b.
The callsigns 1Ø, 2Ø, 3Ø and 4Ø are allocated to the primary subordinate
stations, i.e. squadrons, batteries, companies, etc on unit nets: troops, platoons, etc
on sub-unit nets.
c.
The commander of the unit or sub-unit whose net it is, is allocated the
callsign ØA, with commanders of the primary subordinate stations normally being
1ØA, 2ØA, 3ØA and 4ØA as appropriate.
Other stations are allocated the remaining callsigns.
9.
The aim of this system is to ensure that all nets sound similar. The limited number
of callsigns within the fixed allocation means that on any single net there may be a
duplication of callsigns. To eliminate the confusion this would cause, a callsign indicator
must be used by every station (except control; see below).

14.

(2)

(3)
THIS IS - A proword meaning ‘This transmission is from the station

(4)

CALLSIGN - Identifies the calling station.

and create an exploitable signature.

Message Text. The information to be passed. See Chapter 3 Section 2.

(1)
OUT - meaning ‘This is the end of my transmission to you and no

(2)
OVER - meaning ‘This is the end of my transmission to you and a

CHAPTER 3 - MESSAGE PROCEDURE
SECTION 1 – INTRODUCTION
1.
A message is any thought or idea expressed briefly, in plain or coded language,
and prepared in a form suitable for transmission by a means of communication.
2.
Informal messages account for the majority of information passed on tactical radio
voice nets. An informal message need only contain a call, a message text and an ending
proword.
INFORMAL MESSAGES
3.
Informal messages are transmitted either directly by the originator or passed
verbally or in writing to an operator for transmission. Originators of informal messages for
transmission by an operator should write their message down if verbal instructions are
likely to cause error or confusion.
4.
Written informal messages are not normally drafted on a message form and signed
by the originator or registered in a message centre but are usually passed directly from
originator to operator for transmission.
FORMAL MESSAGES
5.
Tactical voice nets are primarily for the transmission of short operational messages.
On occasions, such nets could be used for passing formal messages by voice, but this
should rarely occur.
6.
When possible, formal messages should be written on the standard message form
(F Sigs 266).
TYPES OF FORMAL MESSAGE
7.

There are two types of formal messages:
a.
Plaindress. A message in which the address section is separate from the
text, using the ‘FROM’, ‘TO’ and, if appropriate, the ‘INFO’ spaces on the message
form.
b.
Codress. An encrypted message in which the entire address component is
concealed within the text.

SECTION 2 - MESSAGE TRANSMISSION
OFFERING A MESSAGE
10.
A preliminary warning transmission to indicate that a message is to follow is known
as an offer. When communications conditions are satisfactory and it is practical to do so,
messages are to be transmitted immediately without an offer.
11.
The habitual offering of messages without valid reasons is poor radio discipline and
must be avoided. Unnecessary offering of messages is counter-productive because
stations will eventually come to expect a preliminary warning transmission and relax their
listening watch accordingly, which, in turn, will reinforce the need to offer messages.
12.
Messages are never to be offered solely to cater for stations which, through failure
to maintain a proper listening watch, often require repetitions of initial calls. The
transmission time saved through not offering messages may appear relatively short in
individual transmissions, but the cumulative effect can significantly speed up net traffic and
reduce vulnerability to intercept.
THE CALLING STATION
13.
An offer is intended to indicate that a message is to follow, and also allow for any
preparation that may be necessary by the receiving station before reception can
commence. A message should only be offered when the calling station has reason to
believe that the called station may be unable to receive or record a message due to
operational circumstances, suspect radio conditions, or for any other valid reason.
14.
Unless the message is a formal message or a formatted message, the proword
MESSAGE followed by the key setting (if appropriate) will be used. This prevents, in the
case of long messages, a duplication of key setting by other stations.
Example.

15.

LONG MESSAGE PROCEDURE
19.
A long message is defined as any message that will take longer than 20 seconds to
transmit. Long messages are divided into convenient sections to allow for satisfactory
reception in manageable stages, and to permit the transmission of more urgent traffic.
Unless it is inappropriate, the limited response procedure should be used.
20.

The following procedure is to be adopted when transmitting a long message:
a.
Long messages should be offered when it is suspected that the receiving
station is not immediately in a position to receive it.
b.
The message is to be divided into sensible sections each of approximately
20 seconds duration or less.
c.
Each section, except the last, is to end with a station being nominated to
acknowledge receipt of that section of the message (within this document:
Limited Response Procedures). The prowords used at the end of each section are
ROGER SO FAR - OVER, which indicates that not only a receipt is required from
the nominated station but that more is to follow. The last section ends with the
proword OVER, indicating that the message is complete and a receipt is required.
Again, a limited response should always be requested if appropriate.
d.
Receiving stations are to receipt each section as nominated unless
repetitions are required.
e.
After receiving a receipt for each section from the nominated receiving
station(s), the sending station is to pause for 5 seconds before resuming
transmission of the next section. This is to allow for the transmission of more urgent
traffic or to allow stations that have not been asked to acknowledge to seek
repetitions.
f.
If there is no interruption during the five seconds pause the sending station is
then to continue with the next section of the message.
g.
The sending station can interrupt its own transmission at any time with WAIT
OUT before making a more urgent call.
h.
After an interruption in the 5 seconds pause between sections a further 5
seconds pause is allowed and, if no more interruptions occur, the sending station
continues with the message.
i.
After any interruption the sending station is to resume its transmission with a
new initial call and a suitable message reference.

33.

c.

d.

Any resulting data has been interpreted accurately.

requests for repetitions, where only the reception of a message is in doubt. Unnecessary
requests for verification can cause delay; the originator of a message may not always be
immediately available at the transmitting station, whereas requests for repetitions can be
dealt with by the operator.

CHAPTER 4 - COMMUNICATION DRILLS
SECTION 1 – INTRODUCTION
GENERAL
1.
Communication drills are designed to make possible the establishment,
maintenance and subsequent alteration of a radio net communications organization to
meet with the requirements of a tactical situation.
2.
The responsibility of the control station for the technical direction of the net and the
efficient operation of all communication drills is absolute. However, substations have a
responsibility for maintaining their equipment in good order, keeping an alert watch on the
correct frequency, and siting themselves in good communicating positions.
3.
Responsibilities of Control include opening and closing the net, giving or denying
permission for stations to join or leave the net, and coordinating all other changes in net
composition and operating techniques.
4.

SECTION 3 - AUTHENTICATION AND CODEWORDS
WHY AUTHENTICATE?
23.
Reliable systems of authentication are necessary to enable stations to distinguish
between genuine and fraudulent transmissions on a radio net.
24.
Authentication procedures are a COMSEC aid designed to prevent the enemy from
infiltrating a radio net, and to allow stations to prove that they are genuine. Everyone who
communicates at a tactical level needs some method of authentication.
25.
The enemy may attempt to enter our nets to issue false orders or induce the
disclosure of information to assist them. Expert deception can be achieved with limited
effort and skill by an enemy operator, which can lead to serious damage and loss of life.
26.
Communications deception is the most prevalent threat to our net security: good
procedures, constant vigilance and authentication are the best ways to counter it.
METHODS OF AUTHENTICATION
27.
For the various methods of authentication and the systems available for use see
Chapter 1. An example of a challenge and reply authentication matrix along with operating
instructions is given at Chapter 7. Operating instructions for the use of other authentication
systems will be issued by the distribution authority with the relevant authentication table.
WHEN TO AUTHENTICATE
28.
Authentication is required whenever the identity of a station is in doubt. It is
important that authentication procedure should be used with discretion, in order to strike a
balance between the protection of our nets and the ability of communications to operate
effectively without undue hindrance. Some examples of instances when authentication
may be used:
a.
When any station suspects the enemy is attempting to intrude on the net for
whatever reason.
b.
When challenged or requested to authenticate by another station, except that
radio silence is never broken solely to answer a challenge. Never answer a
challenge out of the blue.
c.
When a station joins a net (see Chapter 4), makes any form of initial contact
or resumes contact after a long period, or where that station is unexpected or
cannot be recognized.
d.

When a homing signal is requested.

e.

When any unexpected or irrational orders are transmitted.

ANNEX A

BATCO
GENERAL
1.
BATCO is a paper numerical code. It has been specifically designed for ease of
use, therefore the security protection it provides depends on strict adherence to the rules
of use. Unauthorized adaptation of these rules weakens the code and is forbidden.
DESCRIPTION
2.
BATCO is printed in double sided sheet form which fits into a purpose-made wallet;
the latter also incorporates a sliding cursor to aid use. The code can be used on both
formation and unit level nets and the procedure varies for each type of net. Where these
differences occur they are given below. If no difference is indicated the procedure outlined
is applicable to all nets. A single side of a page is normally valid for one day or 24 hour
period. In addition to the code, an authentication table and callsign indicators are printed
on the page.
3.
An example of a BATCO page is given at Appendix 1, which is used to encode all
the examples contained in this pamphlet. The sheet consists of 6 main parts which are:
a.
Heading. The heading gives the edition, page and copy numbers and space
in which to record the period of use. An edition consists of 36 pages.
b.
Settings. On the left-hand side are the cursor settings consisting of 6
columns numbered 2 to 7, each comprising a scrambled alphabet. On unit nets all 6
columns may be used; however, the use of column 7 is reserved and is only to be
used on the authority of the unit commander. A column number and letter, e.g. ‘2I’,
sets the row of alphabet to be used (this is the same row set by ‘3D’, ‘4F’, ‘5O’, ‘6G’
and ‘7O’).
c.

Vocabulary Cards.
a.
Vocabulary cards are a method of representing plain text by substituting and
encoding numbers from 0 to 99. The inclusion of a word or phrase on the
vocabulary card does not mean it has to be encoded every time it appears in
the text of a message.
b.
There is a series of different cards; and index is at Appendix 2 and cards are
reproduced at Appendix 3. Informal cards are not to be produced by formations,
units, arms or schools.
c.
Cards 001-012 constitute the universal Unit Pack for use by all units. They
are supplemented as necessary by the special-to-arm and theatre cards which
make up the balance of the set. Examples of use of the cards are given at Appendix
4.
d.
Card 012 is left blank and is intended to be used for encoding supplementary
information for a specific operation. It can be used for information such as route
names, place/location names, bridge names. Key information or instructions,
objectives, regrouping instructions, redeployment instructions and likely changes in
command. It is not to be used as a routine substitute for other cards and should not
be used as a substitute for nicknumbers (except under the circumstances below).

sheet will be used on all nets in the unit or battlegroup.

7.
The plain text is encoded by reading off either of the two letters underneath the digit

MESSAGE PROCEDURE
8.

General Rules.
a.
Unless the proword GRID, NICK or DIRECTION is used, first tri-gram of a
BATCO message encodes a vocabulary card number. From this, the application of
succeeding bi-grams will be clear, being either vocabulary serial numbers or the
figures in brackets contained in some serials. Confusion may arise when, in the
same message BATCO encoded information is interspersed with plain text. If it is
not possible to avoid this situation by restructuring the message, remain on the last
card identified after plain text has been used.
b.
Some vocabulary serial numbers introduce simple formats like ‘move to
GRID ... (6 figs)’. The missing figures are sent, encoded, immediately following the
vocabulary serial number (see Example 2 in Appendix 4). On decoding the numbers
are inserted in the appropriate place in the format.
c.
When an undefined number is being encoded, i.e. a quantity, the word
‘figures’ is extracted from the Vocabulary Card indicating the start, and the symbol
‘CH’ is used to denote the end of the number and means that the next bi-gram will
be a vocabulary serial number.
d.
In order to maintain the integrity of the code and hence the protection it
provides, the following rules apply:
(1)
No more than 22 digits must be encoded using one setting; if the
message is longer than 22 digits the next setting (or settings) must be used
for the balance of the message. Where the combined length of a question
and its immediate answer is no more than 22 digits the same row may be
used; otherwise the ‘safe’ rule is to use a new key setting for each message.
When a message is longer than 22 digits, the change should be made at a
suitable point in the text before the 22nd digit, e.g. if there are four grid
references to be sent, two should be sent on each setting.
(2)
If, however, a station is preparing a long message on one setting and
discovers that another station has transmitted a message using the same
setting, it is permissible for the first station to transmit its message without
changing the setting.
(3)
Should a station lose track of the progression through the settings it
should ask the control station which setting to use. The prowords SEND
SETTING can be used for this purpose.
(4)
A message may be retransmitted on another net without having to be
re-encoded provided, of course, that the recipient on the second net can
decode it.

e.
Remain on the last card identified in a message after plain text has been

9.
Grid References. Where the only portion of the message to be encoded is a grid
reference, then the proword GRID is used followed by the grid reference directly encoded
in BATCO. For unit use it will not normally be necessary to use the UTM designating
letters to avoid ambiguity of location, but where a UTM is necessary the proword AREA is
used before GRID. The UTM letters follow the proword AREA and should not be encoded.
10.
Nicknumbers. These are encoded in two ways. Either they are encoded in the
same way as grid references using the proword NICK instead of GRID or, for a specific
operation they are allocated to serials within Vocabulary Card 012 and encoded as for a
vocab card serial. (See Examples 5 and 6 in Appendix 4).
11.
Spelling. Spelling with BATCO is authorized only when no suitable vocabulary is
available in vocabulary cards and sending the text clear would give the enemy useful
information; for example, names of servicemen, locations which cannot be sufficiently
described by a grid reference and items specific to a unit’s type or function. Spelling is
achieved through the spelling box, where a letter is identified by its column and row
number, for example A is 23, P is 83, T is 74 and so on. These are then encoded,
introduced by a vocabulary number which means ‘Spelling starts’, and concluded with the
letter representing the ‘CH’ symbol in the plain text on the BATCO sheet.
12.
Sending. In order to facilitate decoding and reduce the signature of some formatted
texts, BATCO should be sent as follows:
a.
The key setting is to be sent immediately before the first tri-gram on that
setting (or the first bi-gram in the case of the prowords GRID, NICK or DIRECTION
being used).
b.
Where encoded text produced bi-grams, e.g. a vocab number or encoded
grid reference, these are to be sent as bi-grams, e.g. ‘123456’ would be sent as ‘AB
CD EF’.
c.
Where the encoded text is a 3, 5 etc figure group, then bi-grams are to be
sent except for the last three digits which are sent as a tri-gram, e.g. the number
‘6,457,320’ would be sent as ‘AB CD EFG’.
d.
Some vocabulary card serials may be amplified by a third figure which
denotes a specific qualification. The resulting tri-gram should be sent in its natural
position in the text.
e.
Where CH or a decimal point stands alone it is combined with the preceding
bi-gram and sent as a tri-gram, e.g. the number ’47.75' would be sent as ‘ABC DE’
(where C = decimal point).

ANNEX B

UNIT AUTHENTICATION (CHALLENGE AND REPLY)
1.
On British Land Forces unit radio nets the method of authentication used is
challenge and reply, based on a matrix of numbers like the one below:

METHOD OF USE
2.
A challenge is made by selecting, at random, a column number and a row number;
this 2 digit number is sent as the challenge.
3.

The correct reply is the 2 digit number at the intersection of that row and column.

Example 1.
Challenge:

38

Reply:

03

4.
The 2 digit number (i.e. the reply) is to be struck off after use, and the challenge that
produces that reply is not to be employed again.
5.
If the chance of successful intrusion is high, the challenge and reply are to be
encoded.
Example 2.

Allocation of Callsign Indicators
a.

Unit Nets.
(1)

CIs are issued on the BATCO sheet for most unit nets.

(2)
CIs are allocated according to SOPs appropriate to the Arm or Service
of the unit, reading from the left of the line.
(3)

For the All-Arms Battle Group the following rules apply:
(a)
Unless two sub-units have the same numerical portion of the
callsign, i.e. two ‘A’ Sqns or two ‘C’ Coy’s, primary CIs are to be used.
(b)
When there is repetition of numbers, the secondary CI is
allocated to the sub-unit which joins the net later, irrespective of
seniority.
(c)
If the sub-units join at the same time, CIs are allocated on
regimental seniority.
(d)
Sub-units then retain that CI allocation for as long as they
remain on the net.
